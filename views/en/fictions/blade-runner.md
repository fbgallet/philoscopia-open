# Blade Runner

*Blade Runner : **Blade Runner***

*Ridley Scott, Hampton Fancher and David Peoples (screenplay), from the novel by Philip K. Dick, 1982*

*The Final Cut (2007), the only version over which Ridley Scott had full control: no voice-over, the unicorn dream included, and an ending that stops at the elevator door. The 1982 versions and the 1992 Director's Cut are not used as evidence.*

Los Angeles, 2019. Manufactured beings, the replicants, almost impossible to tell from humans, serve as slaves in the off-world colonies. They are banned on Earth, and police officers, the blade runners, are tasked with "retiring" them. A few fugitive replicants have come back all the same. The film puts to the test what we think we know about the line between a person and a manufactured thing.

## The problem

**The common belief put to the test** : We think we know what separates a human being from a manufactured object: a human is born, has a past of their own, and no one has set the date of their death in advance; an object is made for a use, and we do with it as we please.

Los Angeles, 2019. The Tyrell Corporation, founded by Eldon Tyrell, makes replicants: living beings engineered in a laboratory, stronger and more agile than humans, used as slave labor in the off-world colonies. They are banned on Earth, and specialized police officers, the blade runners, are tasked with killing them; the official word is "retiring" them. Gaff, a cop who folds little paper figures, brings Rick Deckard, who had left the job, back to his old boss, Bryant: four fugitive replicants have returned to Earth, led by Roy Batty, along with Leon, Zhora and Pris. To kill them, you first have to recognize them, and they look exactly like humans. For this the police have a test, the Voight-Kampff, which takes empathy as its criterion: the subject is asked disturbing questions while a machine watches their eye for an involuntary reaction. The choice is far from obvious. [Descartes](../philosophers/descartes.md) told man from automaton by language and by a reason able to respond to any situation; replicants meet both criteria, and by his lights they would have to be granted a mind. So the test looks for something else: emotion. But the test only says who is a replicant. It does not say why that would make it right to kill them.

If the test does not justify the killing, what they were made for must: in this world function stands in for nature, and nature decides rights. Bryant introduces each fugitive by their function: a combat model, a loader, a trained killer, a "pleasure model". He also explains why they live only four years: their designers feared that, given time, they would come to feel emotions of their own, hate, love, fear, anger, envy. Their death is a safety device, built in so that they remain what they were made for. To show that for human beings ["existence precedes essence"](../glossary/existentialism.md), [Sartre](../philosophers/sartre.md) started from the opposite case, a paper knife: an object whose function is conceived before it is made. Their maker would like replicants to stay that paper knife. But a paper knife is not afraid of dying. If these beings are nothing but their purpose, retiring them amounts to scrapping worn-out tools; if they become someone, nothing distinguishes this "retirement" from murder any more. To decide, one would have to know how to look into them.

Everything then depends on where one looks. The film shifts the examination from the eye one scrutinizes to what that eye has seen. It opens on an eye in extreme close-up, reflecting a city bristling with flames. The test scrutinizes the suspects' irises. In Tyrell's office an owl keeps watch: it is artificial. Batty, for his part, goes to see the man who makes eyes for the company, and tells him that if only he could see what Batty has seen with those eyes… The manufactured eye is not the issue: what counts is what it has seen. And it is the viewer the film puts to the test. Deckard explains to Rachael, a young woman who works for Tyrell, that replicants are like any other machine: either a benefit or a hazard, and if they are a benefit, it is not his problem. The film does not leave us that indifference: it is built to draw from us the very reactions the test means to measure, and to turn them toward the replicants. (Spoilers : When Deckard shoots Zhora in the back, in the middle of the street, as she runs through shop windows, her fall is filmed in slow motion, at length, among the mannequins. When Rachael learns that her memories are not her own, the camera lingers on her tears.) Those who give the test show little of it themselves.

**The question** : If we can make beings like us in every way, what still sets us apart from them?

## Other problems in the work

**Why does Sebastian, who opens his door to them, age too fast?** *(J. F. Sebastian's apartment)*

J. F. Sebastian, a designer at the Tyrell Corporation, lives alone in an empty building, surrounded by automata he has made to keep him company. He suffers from a disease that makes him age too fast: like the replicants, he will not live long. He is the one who takes them in. One may read in this a condition of [pity](../glossary/pitie.md) in [Rousseau](../philosophers/rousseau.md)'s sense, who wrote that we pity in others only the ills we do not believe ourselves exempt from.

## Concepts

- **[Existentialism](../glossary/existentialism.md)** : The film gives a body to the example Sartre set aside: a being made to a plan, whose function precedes its existence. It asks whether such a being can nonetheless make itself through its acts.
- **[Personal identity](../glossary/personal-identity.md)** : If a past can be manufactured, is memory enough to make a person? The film puts the idea to the test.
- **[Revolt](../glossary/revolt.md)** : The refusal of an imposed condition, and what it can found or destroy: the film stages both faces of revolt.
- **[Pity (pitié)](../glossary/pitie.md)** : The empathy the test claims to measure, the film arouses in the viewer, and in its characters it springs from a shared vulnerability.

## What the work answers

The film does not settle the question with an argument. It makes the viewer feel what the test claims to measure, and that empathy goes to the made beings. It does not say whether what they become belongs to them. But it takes away from origin the power to decide what a being is, and what one has the right to do to it.

## What it leaves open

Replicants are organisms, not machines: the question of a bodiless artificial mind stays out of frame, and the empathy they arouse owes much to their resemblance to us. Nor does the film say what consent to death is worth when no choice remains. Finally, it weakens its own defense of manufactured beings.(Spoilers :  At Deckard's, Rachael tries to leave; he blocks the door, pushes her against a wall and dictates words of desire to her, and both the music and the framing cast the scene as a romantic coming-together. The film that asks what one may do to a made being here leaves the replicant subject to the protagonist's desire, without questioning it.)

## Close thinkers

- [Sartre](../philosophers/sartre.md) : The paper knife whose essence precedes its existence: replicants are its living version, and the film asks whether they can escape their purpose.
- [Descartes](../philosophers/descartes.md) : Replicants meet his criteria for telling man from automaton, language and reason; so the film's test looks for something else, emotion. Pris even quotes his "I think, therefore I am".
- [Locke](../philosophers/locke.md) : Personal identity grounded in consciousness of past actions: the film asks what that criterion is worth when a past can be manufactured.
- [Camus](../philosophers/camus.md) : The revolt that founds a "we": the film asks whether the replicants' revolt can go that far.
- [Bentham](../philosophers/bentham.md) : "Can they suffer?": the question the film puts in place of the question of origin.
- [Epicurus](../philosophers/epicurus.md) : A life's worth does not lie in its length: the film tests this consolation against a life deliberately cut short.

## Going further

- Philip K. Dick, *Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?* : The source novel. Empathy is the criterion of humanity there and the androids seem to lack it, though the novel already unsettles that split; the film goes further and gives empathy to the replicants.
- Jean-Paul Sartre, *Existentialism Is a Humanism* : The paper-knife example and the thesis that existence precedes essence: the key to the question of purpose.
- René Descartes, *Discourse on the Method*, Part Five : The two ways of telling man from automaton, language and reason, which the film's test replaces with emotion.
- John Locke, *[Essay Concerning Human Understanding](../works/essay-concerning-human-understanding-locke.md)*, Book II, chapter 27 : Personal identity grounded in consciousness and memory: the theory Rachael's case puts to the test.
- Camus, *[The Rebel](../works/the-rebel-camus.md)*, Chapter I, "The Rebel" : "I rebel, therefore we exist": a revolt that founds a community, like the one that leads Batty to spare his hunter.
- *Blade Runner 2049* (extends) : Denis Villeneuve's sequel pushes the question of origin further: a replicant who may believe he was born, and memories whose owner no one can tell.
- *Frankenstein* (extends) : The model of the creature who comes to call its maker to account, and whom rejection turns murderous.
- *Ex Machina* (contests) : Here empathy becomes the made being's weapon against the one examining it: the film casts suspicion on the trust Blade Runner places in emotion.
- *Ghost in the Shell* (extends) : An entirely artificial body and memories that can be faked: what remains of a self when everything can be manufactured?
