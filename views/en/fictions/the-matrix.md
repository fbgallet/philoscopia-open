# The Matrix

*Lana and Lilly Wachowski, 1999*

*The first film (1999) only; the sequels, which shift the question of choice, are left aside.*

A programmer discovers that the world he lives in is a simulation, and is offered the chance to wake up from it. Beneath the action film, The Matrix raises two questions we think are settled: would we really want to know, if the truth made life harder? And are our choices our own, if everything seems written in advance?

## The problem

**The common belief put to the test** : We usually take it for granted that the world we perceive is real and that, if we were lied to about what matters most, we would want to know: the truth would always be better than an illusion, and our choices would be our own.

*The Matrix* starts from a hypothesis philosophy has entertained since [Descartes](../philosophers/descartes.md): what if everything I perceive were manufactured to deceive me? From this the film draws two questions that guide everything that follows. The first is about truth: would we want to know, if knowing made life harder? The second is about freedom: are our choices our own, if everything seems written in advance?

First question: is a hard truth better than a happy illusion? Thomas Anderson, a programmer by day and a hacker by night under the name Neo, senses that something is wrong with the world. Morpheus, a man the authorities are hunting, reveals to him that this world is a simulation, the Matrix, produced by machines to keep humans asleep. It is Descartes's [evil demon](../thought-experiments/dream-argument.md) made concrete: a power able to manufacture all our sensations. But the film shifts the question. Descartes asked whether we can know; *The Matrix* asks whether we want to know.

Morpheus holds out two pills. The blue one: Neo will wake up in his bed and believe whatever he wants. The red one: he will see "how deep the rabbit hole goes". The image comes from Alice's Adventures in Wonderland, which the film has been following from the start: a message on Neo's screen had already told him to "follow the white rabbit". Morpheus promises nothing but the truth, and Neo chooses it without knowing what it will cost him. The real is a desert: humans grown in pods to power the machines, tasteless food, a war the last free humans can barely sustain. Morpheus puts it in the words of Jean Baudrillard, "the desert of the real", and Neo had hidden his pirated disks in a hollowed-out copy of Baudrillard's Simulacra and Simulation. But that book describes a world where [simulacra](../glossary/simulacra.md) have taken the place of the real, to the point that there is no stable original left to compare them with; the film keeps a real behind the simulation, and makes it hostile. That is what gives the choice its weight: Neo cannot tell himself that the truth will make him happy.

Cypher, one of Morpheus's companions, embodies the other answer. Freed as well, he wonders aloud why he didn't take the blue pill. He has no illusions about the illusion: he knows it is false and misses it all the same. He is like the person [Nozick](../philosophers/nozick.md) imagines facing his [experience machine](../thought-experiments/experience-machine.md), which would let you live, by simulation, every experience you desire: if pleasure is real for the one who feels it, what does it matter where it comes from? For Nozick we would refuse, because we want to do things, not merely have the impression of doing them. Cypher would accept. And he is not alone in preferring the illusion: Morpheus warns Neo that most of the people inside the Matrix are not ready to be unplugged, and that many would fight to defend the system that holds them.

Second question: are our choices our own, if everything is written in advance? Morpheus believes in a prophecy: a chosen one, the One, will come to free humanity, and he thinks he has found him in Neo. Even the name seems to announce it: *Neo* is an anagram of *One*, as if his role were written before him. Yet when Morpheus asks whether he believes in fate, Neo says no: he does not like the idea of not being in control of his life. Morpheus takes him to the Oracle, a woman who foretells the future. Above her kitchen door is a Latin inscription, *Temet nosce*, "know thyself", the Delphic motto [Socrates](../philosophers/socrates.md) made his own. The Oracle adds that being the One is like being in love: no one can tell you, you just know. She will not tell Neo who he is; she sends the question back to him.

One detail of the visit puts the prophecy to the test. The Oracle tells Neo not to worry about a vase; he turns, knocks it over and breaks it. She then asks whether he would have broken it if she had said nothing. The prediction is part of what it predicts, to the point that we can no longer tell whether she foretells the future or produces it. Agent Smith, one of the programs that watch over the Matrix and who interrogated Neo early in the film, sees none of this: for him humans are a disease with predictable behavior, and everything is settled in advance. Between the [free will](../glossary/free-will.md) Neo claims and the destiny Morpheus announces, the film looks for room for a choice that would truly be ours.

The two questions meet, provided we distinguish two senses of freedom. The first is metaphysical: are our choices determined, or written in advance? The second is practical: to be free is to know what one is doing. In this second sense, someone living in an illusion can do as they please, but does not know what they are doing. That is why Morpheus calls the Matrix a prison for the mind: the servitude he wants to free Neo from is not a constraint one feels, it is ignorance. The film slides from one to the other: it shows that knowing gives power (second sense) and treats that as freedom, without saying whether such freedom would hold against a prophecy (first sense). Its answer is therefore more fragile than it looks.

Outside the film, the question is quieter. Everyone has a blue pill: not opening the test results, not asking what a loved one really thinks, holding on to a belief that keeps us standing. And everyone sometimes says of a breakup or a failure that it was "meant to be". Neo looks without knowing what he will see, and refuses to let anyone write his life for him; Cypher, who looked, would rather not have seen. Between them, the film asks a single question: must we know the real to be free, even when it makes life harder?

## Concepts

- **[Hyperbolic doubt](../glossary/hyperbolic-doubt.md)** : The Matrix gives a body to the Cartesian hypothesis of the evil demon: a power able to produce all our perceptions. The film draws a consequence Descartes did not consider: leaving doubt behind not through reason, but through a physical awakening.
- **[Free will](../glossary/free-will.md)** : Neo claims the power to decide his life against any prophecy: the film tests that claim against a future others say they know.
- **[Compatibilism](../glossary/compatibilism.md)** : The Oracle holds prediction and choice together: knowing what someone will do does not stop it from being their own doing. The film can be read as a narrative version of this idea, in which destiny comes true only through a decision.
- **[Hedonism](../glossary/hedonism.md)** : Cypher pushes hedonism to its limit: if only felt pleasure counts, a pleasure whose object is simulated is worth as much as a real one. The film makes us feel both what is coherent in this position and what it leaves out.

## What the work answers

The film answers first through its construction: we discover the Matrix at the same time as Neo, and we cannot take the blue pill, since the film goes on. But it does not rig the alternative. It films reality in dull bluish tones, while the simulation is bathed in the green tint of computer screens, and it gives Cypher time to make his case.

The film's answer is not that truth makes us happy. It is that only truth gives purchase: inside the simulation, Neo is subject to rules he does not understand; once Morpheus tells him that some can be bent and others broken, he starts bending them. The slow-motion shots, where time seems to stop around those who have understood the rules, picture this mastery. It is because we know where we are that we can act. As for the prophecy, the film keeps its answer for the end; until then, the Oracle predicts, but it is Neo who must act.

## What it leaves open

The film makes the illusion easy to refuse: it is manufactured by enemies who exploit humans, and the only one who argues for it is discredited by what he does rather than refuted. The hard case remains open: an illusion freely chosen, which harms no one, like Nozick's machine in its pure form. The film also brushes against those who do not want to be freed: do we have the right to wake someone against their will?

## Close thinkers

- [Plato](../philosophers/plato.md) : The film takes up the pattern of the allegory of the cave: prisoners mistake shadows for the real, and the freed man's eyes first hurt in the light.
- [Descartes](../philosophers/descartes.md) : The evil demon of the Meditations prefigures the Matrix: a deceiver able to produce all our perceptions.
- [Nozick](../philosophers/nozick.md) : His experience machine asks Cypher's question: would we accept a life of simulated pleasures? For Nozick, we want to do things and to be someone, not merely to feel as if we did.
- [Nietzsche](../philosophers/nietzsche.md) : He challenges the film's answer: the will to truth at any cost must itself be questioned, and some illusions are conditions of life.
- [Spinoza](../philosophers/spinoza.md) : For him, freedom does not consist in escaping causes but in understanding them: this is the determinist reading the film fights, and another way of linking freedom and knowledge.
- [Stoicism](../movements/stoicism.md) : The Stoics held, like the Oracle, that destiny is fulfilled through our choices: it leads the willing and drags the unwilling.

## Going further

- Plato, *[Republic](../works/republic-plato.md)*, Book VII, the allegory of the cave : The founding account of liberation from appearances, with what the film takes up (the pain of eyes discovering the light) and what it only touches on (the difficult return to the prisoners, who resist those who want to free them).
- Descartes, *[Meditations on First Philosophy](../works/meditations-descartes.md)*, First Meditation : The evil demon hypothesis, and the question of what withstands doubt when everything we perceive could be manufactured.
- Nozick, *[Anarchy, State, and Utopia](../works/anarchy-state-and-utopia-nozick.md)*, Chapter 3, the experience machine : Three reasons for refusing to plug in, to set against Cypher's plea.
- Nietzsche, *[On Truth and Lie in a Nonmoral Sense](../works/on-truth-and-lie-in-a-nonmoral-sense-nietzsche.md)* : The counterpoint: our truths are illusions we have forgotten are illusions, and the will to truth cannot be taken for granted.
- Jean Baudrillard, *Simulacra and Simulation* : The book appears in the film, hollowed out to hide disks: the thesis of a world where copies have replaced the real.
- *The Truman Show* (extends) : A man discovers that his whole life is staged, and must choose between that reassuring set and an unknown world.
- *The Wild Duck* (contests) : An idealist forces the truth on a family that lived on a lie, with tragic consequences: the play stages the “life-lie” that The Matrix rejects.
- *Minority Report* (extends) : Seers predict crimes before they are committed: does knowing someone's future take away their power to choose?
