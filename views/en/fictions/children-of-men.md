# Children of Men

*Alfonso Cuarón, Alfonso Cuarón, Timothy J. Sexton, David Arata, Mark Fergus and Hawk Ostby, 2006*

*The 2006 film only. P. D. James's novel (1992), which it adapts freely (infertility there is male, the plot and characters differ), is left aside.*

2027: no child has been born on Earth for eighteen years. In a Britain barricaded against refugees, Theo, a jaded civil servant, agrees for money to help Kee, a young refugee, get out of the country. What is left of our reasons to act when humanity has no future, and what do we owe a stranger we were paid to help?

## The problem

**The common belief put to the test** : We usually assume that our reasons for living are our own: our happiness, the people close to us, our projects. Whether humanity disappears after us should change nothing about what matters to us.

The film starts from a simple hypothesis: for eighteen years, no child has been born. From this it draws two questions that guide everything that follows. The first concerns the meaning of what we do, and our reasons to keep going, when humanity has no future. The second concerns what we allow ourselves to do to people in the name of a cause, even the cause of the future.

First question: if no one is to come after us, does what we do still have meaning, and why keep going? The film shows it first through its setting: no apocalyptic landscape, but filthy streets, refugees held in cages, state advertisements for a suicide kit called Quietus. The philosopher Samuel Scheffler starts from the same hypothesis, borrowed from P. D. James's novel, to make a surprising claim: much of what matters to us (inquiring, building, handing things on) assumes, without our knowing it, that others will live after us. Take away this survival of humanity, which Scheffler calls the [collective afterlife](../thought-experiments/collective-afterlife.md), and we would lose the heart to devote ourselves to those activities, even if our own lives were not shortened by a single day. [Hannah Arendt](../philosophers/arendt.md) says something different but points the same way: because we are born, we can begin something new; this is [natality](../glossary/natality.md). In the film's world, where no one is born any more, this capacity to begin seems to die out in the living too.

One scene shows what becomes of this idea among those who govern. Theo, the character the film follows, visits his cousin Nigel, a government minister. Nigel has sheltered Picasso's Guernica and Michelangelo's David in an "Ark of the Arts". Theo asks him what keeps him going; Nigel answers that he simply doesn't think about it. Noah's ark saved living creatures for the world to come; this one saves artworks for a world to come that Nigel would rather not wonder about. He preserves without asking for whom. Theo expects nothing anymore: a former activist, he tells a friend that even if a cure were found, it would be too late.

Yet when his ex-wife Julian, who leads an underground group defending refugees, the Fishes, asks him to help a young refugee, Kee, leave the country, he agrees, for money. Then he keeps going, step after step, far beyond what he had promised. The film gives him neither speech nor conversion: it follows him closely, with a handheld camera, and lets us see that he acts like someone who hopes, without ever saying so. The film's first answer: one can keep acting without believing in the future. But Kee matters to others than Theo: she soon finds herself caught between opposing camps.

Second question: can one serve a cause by using people? The film first raises it through the state. To keep order in a country that believes itself the last one standing, the regime reduces refugees to a security problem: they are locked up, sorted, deported. Theo himself starts by treating Kee as a paid job. [Kant](../philosophers/kant.md) gave the rule: never treat a person merely as a means, even for a just end (one of the formulas of the [categorical imperative](../glossary/categorical-imperative.md)). The word "merely" matters: being paid to help someone does not rule out helping them for their own sake, and that is what Theo discovers along the way. Against those who calculate, such as Syd, a corrupt policeman who charges for every favor, the film sets characters who protect without calculating: Jasper, an old friend of Theo's hidden in the woods, Miriam, who accompanies Kee, and Marichka, a refugee who opens her door to Theo and Kee.

The film puts the second question in its most brutal form: violence. To use force for a cause is to accept that people will pay the price. The state's violence is plain: cages in the streets, round-ups, camps where detainees are beaten and hooded, in images that recall Abu Ghraib. Against it, the Fishes have taken up arms: the state treats them as terrorists; they call themselves the refugees' defenders. Their reasoning recalls [Frantz Fanon](../philosophers/fanon.md)'s: when the order itself is violence, counter-violence can seem the only answer. [Hans Jonas](../philosophers/jonas.md), by contrast, warned against hope turned into a program: he reproached the utopia of the philosopher [Ernst Bloch](../philosophers/bloch.md) for being ready to sacrifice the living to the world it promises. The film does not condemn the Fishes' fight against oppression, but it refuses to make their violence glorious: the fighting is shot in long, almost continuous takes, the camera glued to Theo, with no overview and no heroes. What we mostly see is what war costs those caught in the middle.

The two questions meet in what people do. Nigel preserves without asking for whom; Theo, who expects nothing anymore, nevertheless takes Kee far beyond what his bargain required. Around them, the state files refugees under security risks, Syd charges for his favors, the Fishes take up arms; Jasper, Miriam and Marichka protect without counting the cost. Hence the question that runs through the film: must one believe in a future in order to act, and can one serve it without sacrificing the living to it?

## Concepts

- **[Natality](../glossary/natality.md)** : The film imagines a world deprived of natality in the literal sense, and pushes Arendt's idea to its limit: without births, the capacity to begin, which she did not reduce to a biological fact, seems to die out in the living too. (Spoilers : A child's arrival there regains the weight of a miracle.)
- **[Imperative of responsibility](../glossary/responsibility-principle.md)** : Jonas makes the newborn the archetype of all responsibility: a being whose fragility binds us and whose future depends on us; on this model he extends our responsibility to the humanity to come. (Spoilers : Theo acts on this model, for a vulnerable life that is present.)
- **[Categorical imperative](../glossary/categorical-imperative.md)** : Kant's prohibition on treating a person merely as a means, even for a just end, lies at the heart of the second question. (Spoilers : The film offers a limit case: a child everyone wants to serve, and some would like to make use of.)
- **[The absurd](../glossary/the-absurd.md)** : Acting without any promise that it leads anywhere: this is Theo's situation, and that of Camus's Doctor Rieux. The film asks whether one can call this hope.

## What the work answers

The film answers less with ideas than with gestures. Its answer to the first question is Theo: a man who no longer believes in the future and yet keeps acting, at every point where he could give up. Its answer to the second is acts of care: protecting someone without asking what they will be used for. The camera stays almost always with Theo: we know little more than he does, and nothing tells us whether all is lost or saved. The hope the film leaves standing is therefore not a bet on the outcome, but the choice to act for someone, with no guarantee.

## What the work does

### [Temporal orientation](../axes/time-orientation.md)

The work poses the problem without deciding.

The film poses the problem of the collective afterlife without resolving it. It shows a world in which the absence of a future undoes the present (weariness, Quietus, masterpieces preserved for no one), which supports Scheffler; but it does not say whether our reasons to act really depend on those who will come, or only our will to keep going. The question Theo puts to Nigel is sidestepped.

## What it leaves open

The film leaves out the hardest case: hoping without any sign. It also sidesteps the question put to Nigel. (Spoilers : Its hope rests on an exceptional birth whose cause remains unexplained, and it criticizes the political appropriation of the child while drawing heavily on its symbolic power (the barn, the signs of the cross during the ceasefire).) Finally, Kee and the refugees have little hold on the story: they are its stake more than its subjects.

## Close thinkers

- [Hannah Arendt](../philosophers/arendt.md) : Her thinking of natality makes every birth a beginning, and the child the sign of hope for the world: exactly what the film imagines vanishing.
- [Jonas](../philosophers/jonas.md) : Responsibility born of the fragility of what depends on us, and the critique of a utopian hope ready to sacrifice the present: the film's two poles.
- [Ellul](../philosophers/ellul.md) : He thought about hope in a time of abandonment, when nothing allows one to hope any longer, and examined violence from a Christian standpoint, without justifying it. His hope, however, remains theological, which the film does not claim.
- [Camus](../philosophers/camus.md) : Doctor Rieux in "The Plague" heals without expecting salvation or justification: a figure close to Theo, who acts without believing in the outcome.
- [Kant](../philosophers/kant.md) : The prohibition on treating a person as a mere means separates those who protect people from those who want to use them.

## Going further

- Samuel Scheffler, *Death and the Afterlife*, Lectures 1 and 2 : The philosophical source of the problem: Scheffler borrows from P. D. James's novel the scenario of a sterile humanity to show that many of our values depend on the species surviving.
- Hannah Arendt, *The Human Condition*, Chapter V, "Action", final pages : Natality as the capacity to begin, and birth as "the miracle that saves the world": what the film imagines lost.
- Hans Jonas, *[The Imperative of Responsibility](../works/imperative-of-responsibility-jonas.md)*, Chapter IV (the newborn) and the critique of utopia : The newborn as the archetype of responsibility, and the critique of a hope that would sacrifice the present to the future.
- Jacques Ellul, *[Hope in Time of Abandonment](../works/hope-in-time-of-abandonment-ellul.md)* : A hope that is born when every reason to hope has gone, thought out in a theological framework the film leaves in the background.
- Terry Eagleton, *Hope without Optimism* : The distinction between hoping and forecasting a favorable outcome, which illuminates the film's hope without assurance.
- *The Road* (extends) : A father and his son cross a devastated world "carrying the fire": hope there lies in caring for a child, with no promise about the outcome.
- *Melancholia* (contests) : Facing the end of the world, Lars von Trier's film sets against hope a lucidity without consolation.
- *The Handmaid's Tale* (extends) : In a world where births are becoming rare, a regime seizes women's fertility: the other side of the danger of making the child an instrument.
- *The Children of Men (novel by P. D. James)* (extends) : The source novel, more openly religious and centered on the temptation of power, extends in another way the question of what a humanity without descendants does with its values.
