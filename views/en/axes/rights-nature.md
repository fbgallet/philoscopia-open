# Nature of rights

**Where do rights come from?**

*Others · descriptive (theory) · categorical*

When we speak of "human rights", are they realities inscribed in our nature, rules that societies institute, or tools justified by their benefits? What we can claim, and what no law should be able to take away, depends on it.

## Poles

### Natural rights

Rights are inherent in the person, prior to the state and to positive law: they are discovered, not granted. A law that violates them is unjust, even if perfectly legal.

- The grounding varies: natural law and divine law (Locke), or the dignity and rational autonomy of the person (Kant).

*[Locke](../philosophers/locke.md)*

### Legal positivism

There are no rights but those an authority actually establishes. To speak of natural rights prior to any law is, in Bentham's phrase, "nonsense upon stilts": a right with no institution to secure it is mere wishing.

- Bentham's rejection of natural rights also stems from his utilitarianism, which brings him close to the neighbouring pole.
- Hart, a positivist about the validity of law, nonetheless defended one natural right, to equal liberty.

*[Bentham](../philosophers/bentham.md) · Hart · Kelsen*

### Rights from utility

Rights are neither inscribed in nature nor mere decrees: they are robust protections we recognise because [securing them serves the well-being of all](../axes/ethics-foundation.md). Their force comes from their lasting usefulness, not from a supernatural origin.

*[Mill](../philosophers/mill.md)*

### Political constructivism

Rights are neither natural, nor mere decrees, nor simple calculations of utility: they are [the terms of fair cooperation](../axes/social-contract.md), constructed through deliberation among free and equal persons, and serving as standards of political legitimacy.

*[Rawls](../philosophers/rawls.md) · [Habermas](../philosophers/habermas.md)*

## Live problems

- **The rights of distant peoples** : If some rights belong to every human being by nature, they hold for peoples who know nothing of our law, our faith or our customs, and who have princes of their own. But who decides what human nature is? The very reasoning that shields the stranger has been used to declare whole peoples born to obey, and to justify subjugating them by war, for their own good.
- **The unjust law** : Were the judges who enforced a criminal regime's racial laws applying the law? If so, law and justice are two different things, and the law must be judged from somewhere outside it. If not, we must say where the line falls beyond which a legal order stops being one.
- **Rights against utility** : If we protect rights because respecting them makes everyone better off, what happens the day violating them would serve us better? Is a right that gives way as soon as the sums tip the other way still a right? And can a right that never gives way be justified by its benefits alone?
- **Property before law?** : By what title does land that belonged to no one become mine? Because I worked it, so that it is mine before any law and a state that taxes it takes what I am owed? Or is there a "mine" only where laws and courts uphold it, so that society may redefine what it created?
- **Rights without a state** : A refugee stripped of every nationality ought to be the human-rights bearer par excellence, since nothing is left to them but their humanity. Yet they are the very person who finds that no court will recognize those rights. Do their rights still exist, merely trampled on, or did they never exist without a community to guarantee them?
- **What no vote can take away** : If rights are what free and equal citizens agree to grant one another, could a duly consulted majority abolish one of them? Or are some rights, to speak, to vote, not to be persecuted, the very conditions of genuine agreement, which no agreement can therefore undo?

## Grounding relations

- *[The origin of the social bond](../axes/social-contract.md) : Contractualism* grounds *[Nature of rights](../axes/rights-nature.md) : Political constructivism* : If the legitimate order arises from an agreement among free and equal persons, then rights themselves appear as the terms of a fair cooperation to be constructed, not as givens. Contractualism grounds political constructivism: [Rawls](../philosophers/rawls.md) derives the principles of justice from a rational choice made behind a "veil of ignorance".
- *[Nature of rights](../axes/rights-nature.md) : Natural rights* grounds *[Distributive justice](../axes/distributive-justice.md) : Libertarianism* : If each person holds inviolable rights, beginning with ownership of themselves and of the fruits of their labour, then redistributing by coercion amounts to violating those rights. Natural rights ground libertarianism: for [Nozick](../philosophers/nozick.md), a distribution is just as soon as it results from free exchanges, and the state must confine itself to protecting persons.
- *[Nature of rights](../axes/rights-nature.md) : Natural rights* grounds *[Law and conscience](../axes/political-obligation.md) : Resistance to oppression* : If rights are natural, prior to any law, a power that violates them no longer has title to obedience: the right of resistance follows directly from natural rights. This is [Locke](../philosophers/locke.md)'s argument, for whom the people always retain the power to remove a government that betrays its mission of protecting life, liberty, and property.
- *[Nature of rights](../axes/rights-nature.md) : Political constructivism* grounds *[Distributive justice](../axes/distributive-justice.md) : Egalitarianism* : If rights are the terms of a fair cooperation chosen among equals, a just society must correct the inequalities no one has deserved, those of birth and talent. Political constructivism grounds egalitarianism: [Rawls](../philosophers/rawls.md) admits inequalities only if they also benefit the least advantaged.
- *[Status of norms (metaethics)](../axes/moral-scope.md) : Universalism* grounds *[Nature of rights](../axes/rights-nature.md) : Natural rights* : If there are objective moral norms valid for every rational being, then the person holds rights prior to the state, which no law could legitimately abolish. Moral universalism grounds natural rights: for [Locke](../philosophers/locke.md) as for [Kant](../philosophers/kant.md), a law that violates them remains unjust, even if perfectly legal.
