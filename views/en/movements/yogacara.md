# Yogācāra

*c. 350 – c. 700, living tradition*

A school of Mahāyāna Buddhism whose great systematizers were [Asaṅga](../philosophers/asanga.md) and [Vasubandhu](../philosophers/vasubandhu.md), in India around the fourth and fifth centuries, drawing on earlier sources such as the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra. Its name means "practice of yoga", that is, Buddhist contemplative discipline, whose experience it analyses. It takes up the [emptiness](../glossary/sunyata.md) taught by [Madhyamaka](../movements/madhyamaka.md) and articulates it in the doctrine of the [three natures](../glossary/three-natures.md): the imagined nature (things as we project them), the dependent nature (the conditioned stream of consciousness) and the perfected nature (the dependent emptied of the imagined). Liberation consists in undoing the duality of subject and object, not in making ordinary consciousness an ultimate substance. The logicians Dignāga and Dharmakīrti, often associated with the school, renewed Indian epistemology. In China, Paramārtha transmitted it in the sixth century, then Xuanzang in the seventh, who founded a school of it. Its "idealism" can be read in two ways, as a metaphysical thesis (nothing exists outside the mind) or as an analysis of experience, and the debate still divides scholars. It is still studied in Tibetan curricula.

**Key figures** : [Asaṅga](../philosophers/asanga.md) · [Vasubandhu](../philosophers/vasubandhu.md)

## Positions

### [Nature of reality](../axes/reality-nature.md)

*positioned · explicit in the texts · major*

Idealism 100%

[Vasubandhu](../philosophers/vasubandhu.md) starts from dreams: in them we see objects that are located, lasting and causally effective, without any external object. Nothing therefore requires us to posit things outside consciousness. He adds that atoms, invoked to ground the external world, are incoherent: they can be conceived neither with parts nor without. Everything is [representation-only](../glossary/vijnaptimatra.md) (*vijñaptimātra*). The thesis is explicit; its classification as metaphysical idealism remains disputed.

*Sources : Vasubandhu, Viṃśatikā (Twenty Verses), 2-4 (dreams) and 11-15 (atoms) · Asaṅga, Mahāyānasaṃgraha, II*

### [Nature of the self](../axes/self-nature.md)

*positioned · inferable · major*

Bundle theory 60% · Psychological continuity 40%

To explain the continuity of experience without a permanent self, the school posits a [storehouse consciousness](../glossary/alayavijnana.md) (*ālayavijñāna*): a conditioned stream in which the traces (*vāsanā*) of actions are deposited, ensuring causal continuity from one life to the next without a permanent subject. In keeping with [anattā](../glossary/anatta.md), this stream is not a self; it is the afflicted mind that wrongly takes it for an I.

*Sources : Vasubandhu, Triṃśikā (Thirty Verses), 2-7 · Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra, V*

## Influenced by

- **[Madhyamaka](../movements/madhyamaka.md)** (reinterprets) : [Yogācāra](../movements/yogacara.md) takes up [Madhyamaka](../movements/madhyamaka.md)'s [emptiness](../glossary/sunyata.md) but refuses to read it as sheer nothingness: the doctrine of the three natures distinguishes what is imagined, what depends on something else and what is perfected, and grants consciousness a dependent existence.

## Influence on

- **[Chan / Zen](../movements/chan-zen.md)** (transmits) : Early [Chan](../movements/chan-zen.md) claimed the Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra, a text close to [Yogācāra](../movements/yogacara.md), and kept from that school the idea that awakening consists in recognizing the nature of the mind.
- **[Candrakīrti](../philosophers/candrakirti.md)** (reacts against) : In the Madhyamakāvatāra, [Candrakīrti](../philosophers/candrakirti.md) refutes the [Yogācāra](../movements/yogacara.md) thesis at length: if one denies the external object, one must also deny a consciousness that would exist by itself. This debate fixed, in Tibet, the hierarchy of the schools.
