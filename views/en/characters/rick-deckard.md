# Rick Deckard

*Character from « Blade Runner » (Ridley Scott, 1982)*

A former blade runner, Deckard is forced by his old boss to go back to work and hunt down four replicants who have returned to Earth. He does the job with no visible qualms: to him, replicants are machines like any other. The film follows him from one lead to the next, and it is through what he does, more than what he says, that we see him lose his certainty about where the line runs.

## Positions

### [Essence and existence](../axes/essence.md)

*positioned · inferable · major*

**Declared** : Essentialism 85% · Existentialism 15%

**Practiced** : Existentialism 65% · Essentialism 35%

Before he tests Rachael, Deckard explains that replicants are like any other machine: either a benefit or a hazard, and if they are a benefit, it is not his problem. His words reduce replicants to their function: not a theory of essence, but the logic of his trade, which locates what one is in what one was made for, on the side of [essentialism](../axes/essence.md). (Spoilers : His acts end up contradicting him: he treats Rachael as someone who can choose her life rather than as a product to be retired, and leaves with her even though she is on the list. This recognition remains ambiguous, since he also coerces her.) He never again states his views on replicants; his position shows in what he does, which is why it stays moderate.

*Sources : The conversation with Rachael at Tyrell's, before the test · The final scene, the unicorn and the departure (Final Cut)*

### [Moral circle](../axes/moral-circle.md)

*positioned · inferable · major*

**Declared** : Anthropocentrism 90% · Pathocentrism 10%

**Practiced** : Pathocentrism 60% · Anthropocentrism 40%

At first Deckard kills, on orders, beings who are afraid and who suffer, without asking what he owes them: in practice, in his line of work, only humans count. (Spoilers : He shoots Zhora in the back as she flees. Then Rachael saves his life, Batty spares him after making him feel the fear his kind live with, and he ends up protecting a replicant instead of retiring her.) His acts move him toward [pathocentrism](../axes/moral-circle.md) without his reaching it: his concern goes first to one particular replicant and to the one who spared him, not to every being that can suffer. His early remarks, inferred here rather than stated as a principle, placed him on the opposite side.

*Sources : The pursuit of Zhora · The rooftop · The final scene*

### [Violence](../axes/violence.md)

*positioned · inferable · minor*

**Practiced** : Force as an instrument of politics 70% · Just war 30%

Deckard uses violence as a tool of public order: he kills whoever the administration points him to, and the word "retire" spares everyone from saying "kill". This is [force as an instrument of politics](../axes/violence.md), with a share of defensive justification: the fugitives have killed, and they attack him. (Spoilers : Zhora tries to strangle him before running, but he shoots her in the back as she flees, which takes much of the force out of that justification.)

*Sources : The briefing in Bryant's office · The pursuit of Zhora*
