# The argument from the indivisibility of mind

*Argument for [The enigma of consciousness](../axes/consciousness-nature.md) : Substance dualism*

**The body is made of parts, thought has none: what thinks cannot therefore be identical with what is extended.**

*[Descartes](../philosophers/descartes.md), *[Meditations on First Philosophy](../works/meditations-descartes.md)*, 1641*

## The argument

1. Whatever is material is **divisible**: a body, an organ, a brain can be parted, and each part is still something extended. That is what it means, for a thing, to occupy space.
2. What thinks is not parted in that way: when I deliberate, I do not find one half of me doubting and another willing, but a single **subject** that doubts, wills and feels. Removing a part of a thought does not leave a smaller thought, it leaves nothing.
3. Yet two things that do not share the same properties cannot be one and the same: if one divides and the other does not, then there are two.

**⇒** So what thinks in me is not what is extended: mind is a reality of another kind, and its dependence on the body does not prove it to be a part of it: this is the thesis of substance [dualism](../glossary/dualism.md).

## Deepenings

### Half a consciousness?

*What is lost and divided are the faculties and their objects, not the subject that has them.*

1. One can lose sight, memory, speech: the experience that remains is impoverished in content, never cut in two. A blind person does not have half a consciousness, but a consciousness without images.
2. What divides, then, are the faculties and their objects, never the one to whom all this happens: I cannot so much as picture what half a subject would be, whereas I picture half a brain without difficulty.

**⇒** Indivisibility bears not on the mind's content, which varies and diminishes, but on the subject that undergoes it.

### The argument from clear conception

*I conceive thought including nothing of extension, and extension including nothing of thought: two complete natures, hence separable.*

1. The argument is not the fallacy ascribed to it ("I doubt my body, I do not doubt my thought, so they differ"): what I doubt says nothing of what is, as is shown by the man who doubts that the masked stranger is his brother when he is.
2. What [Descartes](../philosophers/descartes.md) advances is stronger: I have a complete idea of **thought**, into which nothing of **extension** enters, and a complete idea of extension, into which nothing of thought enters. These are not two aspects of one thing, but two natures each of which is conceived whole without the other.
3. But what is conceived separately in this way can exist separately, at least for a power that would need only to will it: nothing in the nature of thought requires a body.

**⇒** So soul and body are really distinct: either could subsist without the other, whatever be the case with their present union.

*[Descartes](../philosophers/descartes.md), *[Meditations on First Philosophy](../works/meditations-descartes.md)*, 1641*
