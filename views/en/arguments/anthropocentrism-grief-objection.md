# Grief for the animal

*Objection to [Moral circle](../axes/moral-circle.md) : Anthropocentrism — attacks the position*

**When your dog suffers, it is for the dog that you ache, not for your own humanity: our duties regarding animals cannot be merely indirect.**

## The argument

1. For anthropocentrism, we have only [indirect duties](../glossary/indirect-duties.md) regarding animals: in its clearest version, [Kant](../philosophers/kant.md)'s, cruelty is wrong because “"he who is cruel to animals becomes hard also in his dealings with men."”
2. But look at what we feel before an injured dog or a mistreated animal: not concern for our own character, but pity for the creature. And we judge guilty whoever tortures an animal in secret, even if it hardens him in no way.
3. Suppose a cruelty without witness, which changes its author in no way: if the wrong lay only in its effects on us, it would be innocent; no one believes that. So it is indeed the animal that is wronged.

**⇒** So the suffering animal counts for its own sake: the moral circle cannot stop at persons without contradicting our firmest judgments.

## Ways out of the objection

- **True: it is for the animal that I ache, and it is the animal that is wronged. Its suffering counts in itself.** : You bring sentient beings into the circle: the step taken by [Bentham](../philosophers/bentham.md), then [Singer](../philosophers/singer.md).
- **That pity does target the animal, but it grounds no rights: we owe it not to make it suffer, not the respect owed to a person.** : You keep the hierarchy, but the animal gains a place of its own in it: duties toward it, without the equality owed to persons.
- **A feeling is not a criterion: we also grieve when a beloved object breaks. Pity says something about us, not about the animal's standing.** : You hold the Kantian line: our emotions bear witness to our humanity, they do not decide who has rights.
