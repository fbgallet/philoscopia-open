# The multiple-realizability argument

*Argument for [Can a machine think](../axes/machine-mind.md) : The substrate does not matter*

**What makes a mental state is the role it plays, not the stuff that realizes it: and the same role can be played by very different materials.**

*Putnam, *"Psychological Predicates" (later retitled "The Nature of Mental States")*, 1967*

## The argument

1. We never ascribe a mental state by inspecting a material: we ascribe it to the **role** it plays in what a being does. It avoids what hurt it, hesitates, learns, corrects itself. That is how we recognize a dog's pain and a child's fear.
2. Yet the same role is played by very different materials and architectures, which is called [multiple realizability](../glossary/multiple-realizability.md): the octopus, whose nervous system is nothing like ours, hesitates and learns; and in humans themselves, after a lesion, a function relocates elsewhere in the brain without the corresponding experience disappearing.
3. What varies while the mental state stays the same is not what constitutes it: if the substrate can vary while pain remains pain, it is not the substrate that makes the pain.
4. What remains is what did not vary: the **organization**, that is, a network of relations between what comes in, what is transformed within, and what goes out. Nothing in that organization mentions carbon, neurons, or the living.

**⇒** So demanding a particular material is not a philosophical requirement but a **substrate prejudice**: whatever realizes the organization thinks, silicon included.

## Deepenings

### The octopus and plasticity

*Nature itself realizes mind in more than one way: in the octopus, and even within a human brain that reorganizes itself.*

1. The octopus has neurons, but its nervous system is spread through its arms, with nothing resembling our architecture. It nonetheless solves problems, recognizes people, learns by trial: a lineage separated from ours for six hundred million years realizes the same functions otherwise.
2. And without leaving our own species: in a child whose hemisphere is removed, speech and memory re-establish themselves in the other. It was therefore not that particular tissue that carried those states, since another carries them.
3. Demanding the material of the human brain would mean denying pain to everything that is not us: biology has already ruled against that demand.

**⇒** Multiple realizability is not a philosopher's hypothesis: it is a fact of nature, which the machine merely extends.

### We never have any other proof

*I grant thought to my fellows on their words and gestures alone: denying it to whatever does the same would be a double standard.*

1. Of another's mind I never have direct access: I perceive only bodies that speak and act. On that basis alone I grant everyone an inner life, without its striking me as risky.
2. If a machine holds its place in those same exchanges, answering, explaining itself, correcting itself, then demanding an extra proof applies to it a criterion applied to no one else.
3. In the [imitation game](../thought-experiments/turing-test.md), [Turing](../philosophers/turing.md) does not claim to prove that it thinks: he points out that we have no other criterion, and that failing to produce one, the refusal is merely a privilege we grant ourselves.

**⇒** Denying thought to the machine requires a criterion we apply to none of our fellows.

*[Turing](../philosophers/turing.md), *"Computing Machinery and Intelligence"*, 1950*
