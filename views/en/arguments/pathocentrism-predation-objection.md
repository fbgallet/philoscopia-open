# The fox and the rabbit

*Objection to [Moral circle](../axes/moral-circle.md) : Pathocentrism — attacks the position*

**If all suffering counts, whatever its cause, then wild nature is a slaughter we ought to stop: the criterion goes too far.**

## The argument

1. Pathocentrism holds that suffering is bad in itself, for whoever feels it: what counts is the pain, not who causes it.
2. Yet nature is full of suffering that no one causes: the rabbit eaten alive, the gazelle under the lioness, the billions of young that die of hunger or parasites. “"Nearly all the things which men are hanged or imprisoned for doing to one another are nature's every day performances,"” wrote [John Stuart Mill](../philosophers/mill.md).
3. If comparable suffering calls for comparable consideration, it seems we ought to intervene: protect the prey, feed the starving, perhaps do away with predators. Many find this conclusion absurd or dangerous.

**⇒** So either pathocentrism accepts an extravagant conclusion, or suffering alone does not ground our duties: we must take into account who causes it, or the balance of living systems.

## Ways out of the objection

- **Fair enough: predation is part of the balance of life. What counts is the health of living systems more than each instance of suffering.** : You shift your gaze from the individual to the whole: the step of ecocentrism, for which predation is not an evil but a function.
- **Our first duty is not to cause suffering, not to repair all of nature: my criterion governs what we do, not what the fox does.** : You distinguish causing suffering from allowing it: the fox is not a moral agent, and our duties bear first on our own acts.
- **The conclusion is unsettling, but not absurd: where we can reduce the suffering of wild animals without wrecking everything, by treating or vaccinating them, we should.** : You accept the consequence: the position of those who make wild-animal suffering a moral cause in its own right.
