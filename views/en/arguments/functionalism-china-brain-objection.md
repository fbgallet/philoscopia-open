# The China-brain objection

*Objection to [Can a machine think](../axes/machine-mind.md) : The substrate does not matter — attacks the reasoning*

**If organization suffices, then a crowd passing messages thinks — and one no longer sees what one has said.**

*Block, *"Troubles with Functionalism"*, 1978*

## The argument

1. The argument holds that any realization of the right organization suffices, whatever the substrate: that is the decisive step, and it comes with no restriction.
2. Let us push it through: suppose the entire population of a country, equipped with telephones and instructions, reproduces exactly the causal relations of your neurons, one person per neuron. The organization, that **network of causal roles**, is the same, speed apart.
3. One would then have to say that a **subject** appears above that crowd, with its pains and memories, of which no participant has the faintest idea. The conclusion is not merely strange: nothing in the argument says why it would be true.
4. Either one accepts it, and "thinking" no longer means what we meant; or one refuses it, and then something besides organization matters: speed, material, the way the parts hold together.

**⇒** [Multiple realizability](../glossary/multiple-realizability.md) proves that many substrates will do, not that any will: the argument takes that step without justifying it.

## Ways out of the objection

- **If organization does not suffice, perhaps flesh is required: a living being, not an assembly.**
- **What the crowd lacks are the causal powers of a real brain, not life: an artifact of another kind could have them.** : This is [Searle](../philosophers/searle.md)'s position: refusing the program without reserving thought to the living.
- **Our discomfort comes from scale and slowness, not from an argument: the China brain would think, very slowly.** : The functionalist reply: our intuitions are calibrated on objects our own size, and have never been a good guide here.
