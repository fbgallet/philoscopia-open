# The self-censorship argument

*Argument for [Political liberty](../axes/political-liberty.md) : Republican non-domination*

**To be free is to depend on no one's arbitrary power: an unchecked power is enough to enslave, even if it never acts.**

*Pettit, [Skinner](../philosophers/skinner.md), *Republicanism: A Theory of Freedom and Government*, 1997*

## The argument

1. Living exposed to another's good will changes conduct without any interference being needed: whoever can lose everything at another's whim learns to please, to keep quiet, to anticipate the wishes of the powerful.
2. This **self-censorship** is a real loss of liberty: options that no law and no one forbids become practically unusable, out of prudence.
3. Present [non-interference](../glossary/negative-liberty.md) changes nothing here: it rests on discretionary permission, not on an enforceable right, and so remains revocable; what weighs is not what the powerful do, but what they could do with impunity.
4. Only protections independent of anyone's good will (laws, enforceable rights, **counter-powers**, avenues of appeal) undo this hold: they do not make the powerful better, they subject their power to check and contest.

**⇒** Liberty is a status: not living under **arbitrary power**, that is, a power that could be exercised without possible check or contest.

## Deepenings

### The eyeball test

1. A free society, Pettit proposes, is one where anyone can look anyone else in the eye without fear or deference: without having to lower one's gaze before an employer, a husband, or an official, or to monitor their moods.
2. The test makes non-domination observable: wherever strategies are needed to shield oneself from another's good will, there is domination, however gentle the masters.

**⇒** Liberty is measured by the posture each person can hold before others.

*Pettit, *On the People's Terms*, 2012*
