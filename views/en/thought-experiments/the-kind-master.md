# The slave of the kind master

*Philip Pettit, after the republican tradition, *Republicanism: A Theory of Freedom and Government*, 1997*

Imagine a slave who has fallen to a master of even, benevolent temper: never a brutal order, never a punishment; he lets him live, work, and love as he pleases. No interference, then, from one end of his life to the other. His neighbor is a poor citizen, protected by the law. If liberty is nothing but the absence of hindrance, the well-treated slave is as free as the citizen, perhaps freer.

**Is the slave of the kind master free?**

**Axes at stake** : [Political liberty](../axes/political-liberty.md)

## What it does in the debate

### Objects to [Political liberty](../axes/political-liberty.md) : Negative liberty — attacks the position

If liberty is mere non-interference, the gentle master's slave is free; even the defender of negative liberty hesitates to grant it.

**The argument it makes**

1. By hypothesis, the kind master never interferes: by the criterion of non-interference, his slave is fully free.
2. Yet the defender of negative liberty himself hesitates to count this slave as a free man: servitude is precisely what the status of a free man is meant to rule out.
3. What the slave lacks is not the absence of present hindrance but protection against **arbitrary power**, that is, power uncontrolled and uncontestable: his liberty depends on a mood, not on a right.

**⇒** Actual non-interference is not enough to define liberty: a definition must be able to say why the kind master's slave is not free.

- **You are right: to be free is to be protected from arbitrary power, not merely spared by it.** : You join the republican tradition: liberty is a guaranteed status, not a stroke of luck.
- **The objection shows only that non-interference must be guaranteed by laws, not that liberty needs another definition.** : An intermediate position in practice: guaranteed non-interference edges toward non-domination without taking the step.
- **The lucky slave can act as he pleases: what he lacks is security, which is a different good from liberty.** : Paley's and the liberals' reply: distinguish liberty from the security of liberty, without denying that security's worth.

**Puts to the test** : [The plurality-of-ends argument](../arguments/negative-liberty-pluralism.md)

**Possible stances** :

- Yes: nothing and no one prevents him from acting as he pleases; his condition is more enviable than that of many free men.
- No: his entire life hangs on another's good will, which can take everything back at any moment.
- No, but for another reason: with no part in common affairs, he lives under rules he never helped author.
